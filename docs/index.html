Content Opportunity Scoring

A signal-based research study for prioritizing content pages for refresh or review using observable search-performance data.

Abstract Question Data Methodology Results Limitations Recommendations Reproducibility

Abstract

This study asks which observable search-performance signals can be combined into a repeatable score to prioritize content pages for refresh or review.

The analysis uses content, search, traffic, engagement, age, and trend-related signals and evaluates them using descriptive signal tests and a Random Forest classification model.

Signal audits found strong right-skew in several variables, so medians, grouped comparisons, and directional interpretation were used rather than relying only on averages.

Under client-grouped validation, the Random Forest achieved 57.24% accuracy compared with a 54.21% majority-class baseline, while the original random split produced 70.13%.

The results support a repeatable decision-support workflow for content review, but they do not establish causal effects or guarantee performance on unseen clients.

1. Research Question

Research Question

Which observable search-performance signals can be combined into a repeatable score to identify content pages that should be prioritized for refresh or review?

Decision Supported

The analysis is designed to help prioritize which content pages should be reviewed first based on observed search-performance patterns.

The resulting score is a decision-support ranking. It is not a claim about Google's ranking algorithm and does not establish a causal effect of refreshing content.

2. Data

This study uses the FlyRank internship warehouse release on Hugging Face, with the analysis centered on the fact_content_daily_performance table.

The analysis uses public-safe hashed identifiers and aggregate search-performance measures rather than client names, domains, URLs, private queries, or raw exports.

Primary Signals

  • search_volume — observed search demand
  • competition — observed competition level
  • word_count — content length
  • impressions_90d — observed search visibility
  • clicks_90d — observed search clicks
  • sessions_90d — observed recent traffic
  • content_age_days — content age
  • ctr — click-through rate
  • avg_position — observed search position
  • engagement_rate — observed engagement
  • scroll_rate — observed scrolling activity
  • trend_pct — observed performance movement

Exclusions

Sparse AI-referral fields were excluded from the primary opportunity analysis because many observations contain zero values and the signals are not sufficiently dense for the baseline workflow.

Client-identifying information, domains, URLs, private queries, credentials, and raw warehouse exports are not included in the public analysis.

Data Limitations

The warehouse contains observed search and analytics measurements, but it does not establish why performance changed. It cannot by itself identify causal effects of refreshes, algorithm changes, competitor activity, seasonality, or marketing campaigns.

3. Methodology

Opportunity Definition

A content opportunity is treated as a page whose observed signals suggest that it may deserve earlier review or refresh consideration.

There is no direct ground-truth field stating that a page definitely required a refresh. Therefore, the opportunity score is a prioritization tool rather than a verified prediction of refresh success.

Signal Audit

Several numeric features showed strong right-skew and heavy tails. Extreme observations can strongly affect averages, so the analysis uses medians, grouped comparisons, and directional interpretation where appropriate.

Three signals were tested:

  1. Trend direction versus recent sessions.
  2. Engagement rate versus recent sessions.
  3. Search volume versus impressions.

All three tests were classified as MIXED.

Baseline

The baseline for classification validation is the majority-class prediction on the same client-grouped test set.

30,000 Total observations
16,262 Declining observations
13,738 Non-declining observations

The majority class represents approximately 54.21% of observations.

Proposed Model

A Random Forest classifier was evaluated as a stronger modelling approach for identifying declining content. The model used content, search, traffic, engagement, age, and categorical features while excluding identifiers and target-derived trend fields.

Validation Design

The original Week-5 random split produced 70.13% accuracy.

A client-grouped split was then performed using GroupShuffleSplit. Records belonging to the same client were kept together rather than allowing the same client to appear in both training and testing groups.

The client-grouped evaluation produced 57.24% accuracy.

Leakage Checks

The target label was created from trend_direction. Target-derived and closely related fields were excluded:

  • trend_direction
  • trend_pct
  • impressions_last_30d
  • clicks_last_30d
  • sessions_last_30d
  • impressions_prev_30d
  • clicks_prev_30d
  • sessions_prev_30d
  • client_id
  • content_id

Key Findings

  • The model performed modestly above the majority-class baseline under client-grouped validation.
  • The random-split result was substantially higher than the client-grouped result.
  • The grouped result provides a more conservative estimate of performance on unseen clients.
  • The tested signals provide directional information that can support content review prioritization.
  • The evidence is not strong enough to claim equal generalization to every new client or content portfolio.

5. Limitations & Honest Framing

  • The dataset does not contain a verified ground-truth label for whether a content page actually needed a refresh or whether a refresh successfully improved performance.
  • Several search and traffic features are highly skewed, meaning extreme observations can influence averages and model relationships.
  • Accuracy decreased from 70.13% under a random split to 57.24% under a client-grouped split.
  • The analysis measures associations in observed data and cannot establish that word count, content age, engagement, trend, or another feature causes search-performance changes.
  • Recommendations are intended for decision support. Human review remains necessary before content changes are made.
Important: These findings should be interpreted as observed, measured and directional evidence. They should not be treated as causal claims about search algorithms or guaranteed refresh outcomes.

6. Ranked Recommendations

Priority 1 — Review declining pages

Pages showing declining trend signals should receive early review, especially when recent sessions or other performance indicators also appear weak.

Action: inspect the page before deciding whether to refresh, improve, merge, or monitor it.

Priority 2 — Review pages with weak engagement

Pages with weaker engagement signals can be reviewed for relevance, structure, readability, and alignment with user intent.

Action: review content quality and user experience before making changes.

Priority 3 — Review high-demand pages with weak visibility

Pages associated with stronger search demand but weaker observed visibility may represent opportunities for further investigation.

Action: review search intent, content coverage, internal linking, metadata, and current positioning.

Priority 4 — Review aging content

Older content can be monitored for potential refresh opportunities, especially when age is combined with declining or weak performance signals.

Action: compare older pages against recent performance before selecting a refresh action.

Priority 5 — Monitor mixed-signal pages

Pages with mixed or conflicting signals should remain in a monitoring queue rather than receiving an automatic action.

Action: collect additional observations and use human judgment before making a major content change.

Conclusion

The analysis shows that observable search-performance signals can provide useful directional information for content review prioritization.

However, validation results demonstrate that performance estimates depend strongly on the validation design. The client-grouped result provides a more conservative view than the original random split.

The final workflow should therefore be used as a repeatable decision-support system that helps a content team decide what to inspect first, while leaving the final content decision to human review.
