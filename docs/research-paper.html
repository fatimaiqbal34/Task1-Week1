Content Opportunity Scoring for Search Review Prioritization

A public-safe analysis of observable search-performance signals for prioritizing content pages for review or refresh.

Author: Fatima Iqbal   ·   Lane: Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring

Paper Contents

Abstract 1. Introduction 2. Data 3. Methodology 4. Results 5. Limitations 6. Recommendations 7. Reproducibility 8. Data Credit

Abstract

This study asks which observable search-performance signals can be combined into a repeatable score for prioritizing content pages for refresh or review.

Using anonymized FlyRank internship search and analytics data, the analysis evaluates search, traffic, engagement, content-age, and trend signals.

A Random Forest classifier is compared with a majority-class baseline using client-grouped validation.

The Random Forest achieved 57.24% accuracy compared with 54.21% for the majority baseline, while the original random split achieved 70.13%.

The results are treated as directional decision support rather than a causal explanation of Google's ranking system or a guarantee that refreshing content will improve performance.

1. Introduction / Problem Statement

Content teams may have many pages that could potentially be reviewed, while having limited time to inspect every page in equal detail.

This project investigates whether observable search-performance signals can help prioritize which content pages should receive human review first.

Research Question

Which observable search-performance signals can be combined into a repeatable score to identify content pages that should be prioritized for refresh or review?

Decision Supported

The resulting score is intended to support content review prioritization based on observed search-performance patterns.

It is a decision-support ranking, not a claim about Google's ranking algorithm and not evidence of a causal effect from refreshing content.

2. Data

This study uses the FlyRank internship warehouse release, with the analysis centered on the fact_content_daily_performance table.

The table is organized at daily content-page level. The analysis uses public-safe hashed identifiers and aggregate search-performance measures rather than client names, domains, URLs, private queries, or raw exports.

Primary Signals

  • GSC impressions — observed search visibility.
  • GSC clicks — observed search clicks.
  • GSC average position — observed average search position.
  • GA4 pageviews — observed page traffic where available.
  • Scroll events — observed engagement activity.

Excluded Signals

Sparse AI-referral fields were excluded from the primary opportunity analysis because many observations contain zero values and the signals were not sufficiently dense for the main analysis.

Data Safety

No client-identifying information, domains, URLs, private queries, credentials, or raw warehouse exports are included in this public research artifact.

Public-safe framing: Missing or unavailable measurements are not treated as evidence of poor content performance.

3. Methodology

The project uses a repeatable content-opportunity scoring approach. The goal is to identify pages that deserve earlier human review rather than predict Google's ranking system.

Opportunity Definition

A content opportunity is treated as a page whose observed signals suggest that it may deserve earlier review or refresh consideration.

There is no direct ground-truth field stating that a page definitely required a refresh. Therefore, the opportunity score is a prioritization tool rather than a verified prediction of refresh success.

Signal Audit

The distributions of the main numeric fields were inspected before building recommendations. Several features showed strong right-skew and heavy tails.

Because extreme observations can strongly affect averages, the analysis uses medians, grouped comparisons, and directional interpretation where appropriate.

Baseline

The baseline for classification validation is the majority-class prediction on the same client-grouped test set.

54.21% Majority baseline

The dataset contained 16,262 declining observations and 13,738 non-declining observations.

Proposed Model

A Random Forest classifier was evaluated as a stronger modelling approach for identifying declining content. The model used content, search, traffic, engagement, age, and categorical features while excluding identifiers and target-derived fields.

Validation Design

The original Week-5 random split produced 70.13% accuracy. To test whether that result was optimistic, a client-grouped split was performed using GroupShuffleSplit.

Records belonging to the same client were kept together rather than allowing the same client to appear in both training and testing groups.

Leakage Checks

Because the target label was created from trend_direction, target-derived or closely related fields were excluded.

Key Findings

  • The model performed modestly above the majority-class baseline under client-grouped validation.
  • The random-split result was substantially higher than the client-grouped result.
  • The grouped result provides a more conservative estimate of performance on unseen clients.
  • The tested signals provide directional information that can support content-review prioritization.
  • The evidence is not strong enough to claim that the model will generalize equally well to every new client or content portfolio.

5. Limitations & Honest Framing

This analysis has several important limitations.

  • The dataset does not contain a verified ground-truth label for whether a content page actually needed a refresh or whether a refresh successfully improved performance.
  • The declining-content label is therefore a proxy for review prioritization.
  • Several search and traffic features are highly skewed, and extreme observations can influence averages and model relationships.
  • Model accuracy decreased from 70.13% under a random split to 57.24% under a client-grouped split.
  • The analysis measures associations in observed data. It cannot establish that content age, engagement, trend, word count, or another feature causes search-performance changes.
  • Human review is still required before making content, refresh, merge, rewrite, or removal decisions.
Honest claim: This work provides measured, directional, decision-support evidence. It does not predict Google's algorithm and does not establish causal refresh impact.

6. Ranked Recommendations

The analysis supports a review-prioritization workflow rather than an automatic content decision system.

PRIORITY 1

Review Declining Pages

Pages showing declining trend signals should receive early review, especially when recent sessions or other performance indicators also appear weak.

Recommended action:

Inspect the page before deciding whether to refresh, improve, merge, or monitor it.

PRIORITY 2

Review Weak-Engagement Pages

Pages with weaker engagement signals can be reviewed for content relevance, structure, readability, and alignment with user intent.

Recommended action:

Review content quality and user experience before making changes.

PRIORITY 3

Review High-Demand Pages With Weak Visibility

Pages associated with stronger search demand but weaker observed visibility may represent opportunities for further investigation.

Recommended action:

Review search intent, content coverage, internal linking, metadata, and current positioning.

PRIORITY 4

Review Aging Content

Older content can be monitored for potential refresh opportunities, especially when age is combined with declining or weak performance signals.

Recommended action:

Compare older pages against recent performance before selecting a refresh action.

PRIORITY 5

Monitor Instead of Automatically Changing

Pages with mixed or conflicting signals should remain in a monitoring queue rather than receiving an automatic action.

Recommended action:

Collect additional observations and use human judgment before making a major content change.
